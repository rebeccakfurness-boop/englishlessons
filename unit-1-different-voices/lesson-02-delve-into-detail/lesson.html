Teach · The detective’s method

Clue + What You Know

A detective never guesses. They take something that is actually there, add what they already know about the world, and reach a conclusion. Reading works exactly the same way, and it has a name.

The clue

Something really written in the text. You can point at it and quote it.

+
What you already know

About people, places, stories, life. You bring this yourself.

=
An inference

A sensible conclusion the writer never actually wrote down.

Try it with something ordinary

“She unlaced her shoes at the door and left them beside three other pairs.”

Clue: three other pairs of shoes. What you know: people take shoes off at the door in many homes, and one person has one pair. Inference: she is arriving somewhere other people are already waiting, and she isn’t the guest of honour.

Nowhere does it say “other people were there”. You worked it out. That is inference.

The rule that gets you the marks: an inference without a quotation is only an opinion. Always finish your answer with “…because the text says ‘______’.”

Read · Together, out loud

The Last Dance

A prologue in two halves. Read it twice: once straight through, then once slowly, looking for anything the writer has planted on purpose.

Prologue

Three summers before the lights went out.

The sand was still warm when Mira took off her shoes and stepped into the circle. Somewhere behind her a drum began, slow at first, the way her grandmother always started, as though the music had to be coaxed out of the ground rather than played.

She had danced on this beach every year since she could walk. She knew the count. She knew the turn. She knew that when the drum quickened, the whole village would move in together like one long breath.

What she did not know was that this would be the last time.

If you are reading this, then you found the box, and the shoes are inside it. Wear them. Dance the old way, the way I showed you, with your heels in the sand and your chin up. Do not look for me in the places I told you about. Look for me in the places I never mentioned. I am sorry I could not explain before I left. There was not enough time, and there were too many people listening.

M.

Notice the shape before the detail. Two parts, and they don’t match. The first is told about Mira by someone else: “she”, “her”. The second is in italics, and someone is speaking straight to you: “I”, “you”. Who is each one? That question is the hook.

Teach & do · Mood

How the Writing Feels

Mood is the feeling a piece of writing gives the reader. The writer builds it deliberately, out of word choices. Choose a part, then tap the mood words you think fit it.

Tap a mood word to test it against the text.

Two moods in one prologue is not a mistake. The warm opening is there so that the note feels colder by comparison. Writers set up a feeling in order to break it.

Activity · Change the mood

Same Moment, Three Feelings

Here is the opening sentence again, rewritten three times. The event never changes: only a few words do. Match each version to the mood it creates.

Original: “The sand was still warm when Mira took off her shoes and stepped into the circle.”

Notice how few words had to change. Mood is built in the small choices: a verb, an adjective, one extra detail at the end of the sentence.

Teach · Three ways to open

Backwards, Forwards, or All-Knowing

Prologues play with time. There are three moves a writer can make. Read each opening and decide which one it is.

⏪ Flashback

Takes you back to something that already happened, to explain how things got here.

⏩ Foreshadowing

Hints at something ahead. A flash forward builds suspense by showing you a piece of the ending.

👁️ All-knowing narrator

A voice that knows more than the characters do and tells you things they cannot know.

Back to our prologue: it uses two of the three at once. “What she did not know…” is an all-knowing narrator: Mira cannot know it, but the voice telling the story does. And “Three summers before the lights went out” is foreshadowing: something goes badly wrong, and you now have to keep reading to find out what.

Take the Word Apart

You have used the word prologue for two lessons. It is actually two ancient Greek words stuck together, and once you can see them, you can unlock dozens of other words.

Greek
pro
before
+
Greek
logos
word, speech
→
English
prologue
the words before

Which means the opposite exists too: epi (in addition) + logos → epilogue, the words after the story ends.

The logos family: tap to take each one apart

Five more prefixes: guess before you flip

One for a dancer. Choreography comes from Greek khoreia (dancing) + graphein (to write). A choreographer is literally someone who writes a dance down. Every time you learn a routine, you are reading something that was written, just not in letters.
