🗣️ Teaching Script — tutor only

Read Like a Detective

Lesson 2 · Cambridge Primary English Stage 6, Unit 1: Different voices – different times · 40 minutes, one-to-one online · Written for a tutor teaching this content for the first time

🗣️ Script

Before you start

Your student has done one lesson on prologues. They know a prologue sits before chapter one, that its job is to hook the reader, and that writers set a scene using characters, environment and time — that was their worksheet. They have described a scene. They have not yet read one as a detective.

That is the whole job today: moving from "what does this say" to "what does this suggest, and where exactly does it say so". It is the single biggest step in Stage 6 reading, and it is where marks are won or lost in comprehension papers.

Where this sits in the curriculum. Cambridge Primary English Stage 6 (0058), Unit 1 Different voices – different times, Session 1.2 Delve into detail. The coursebook goal for this session is: "explore the prologue detail and work with prefixes and word origins." The objectives this lesson targets:
  • 6Ri.14 — "Support answers to questions with reference to, or quotations from, one or more points in a text." (the clue hunt, and the quiz's last question)
  • 6Rv.04 — "Comment on a writer's choice of language, demonstrating some awareness of the impact on the reader." (the mood work)
  • 6Rv.02 — "Explore word origins and derivations, including the use of words from other languages." (prologue / logos / the prefixes)
  • 6Rv.01 — "Deduce the meanings of unfamiliar phrases from their context." (supporting, throughout)
Why the text isn't from the coursebookThe coursebook builds this session on the prologue to East. The prologue here — The Last Dance — is written for this lesson instead, so it is fresh for the student, so it uses dance, a beach and travel, and so the clues are planted at exactly the density a 40-minute lesson needs. It deliberately copies the coursebook's shape: a third-person scene followed by a first-person note in italics, with two different moods. If you later want to run the coursebook text alongside it, everything taught here transfers directly.
The one habit to enforce all lessonEvery time the student gives you an answer, ask: "Where does it say that?" Do not accept an answer without a quotation, even a correct one. By the end of the lesson you want them reaching for the text before you have to ask. This is 6Ri.14 and it is worth more marks than any other single habit in Stage 6.

1

Welcome & what's coming

2 min0:00 → 0:02

Screen 1 of the lesson artifact. Share your screen, or send them the link and drive it yourself — either works, but you click, so the pace stays yours.

Say something like

"Last time you told me what a prologue is and you wrote some beautiful descriptions — characters, the place, the time. Today we're doing the opposite job. Instead of writing a scene, we're going to take one apart."

"I've got a prologue here with things hidden in it. Not hidden in a silly way — hidden the way real writers hide things, where it's right in front of you and you only see it if you're looking. By the end of today you're going to be very hard to fool."

Read the five outcomes in the teal box out loud together. Then read the gold box — the Cambridge objective — and put it in your own words:

Say

"Here's the rule for today, and it's the rule for every reading test you will ever do: never just tell me what you think. Show me where you got it from."

2

Warm-up: what you already know

3 min0:02 → 0:05

Four flip cards. Rule: they answer out loud first, then you tap to reveal. Don't let them tap before answering — the guessing is the learning.

CardWhat you're listening for
What is a prologue?"Before chapter one" and "to hook you". If they only say "at the beginning", push: "Before or after chapter one?"
Setting the sceneCharacters, environment, time — straight off their worksheet. They should get all three. If they miss one, it's usually time.
Which is stronger?The sensory one. Ask why. Best answer: because the reader feels it themselves instead of being told.
EvidenceA new word. Let them guess. Anything near "proof" is good. Then give them the reading definition: the exact words from the text.
Keep it movingThree minutes for four cards is roughly 45 seconds each. If they are flying, spend the saved time on the clue hunt in stage 5 — that is the heart of the lesson and it can absorb anything you give it.
3

Teach: clue + what you know = inference

4 min0:05 → 0:09

This is the concept the whole lesson hangs on. Teach it slowly. The three-box equation on screen does the explaining — your job is the example underneath it.

Say

"A detective never guesses. A guess is when you make something up. A detective takes something that is really there, adds what they already know about the world, and works out something nobody told them. Readers do exactly the same thing, and it's got a name — inference."

Now the shoes example on screen. Read the sentence out and stop. Don't explain it — ask:

Ask

"Three other pairs of shoes by the door. What does that tell you that the sentence never actually says?"

Target answer: other people are already there. If they get it instantly, push one step further: "And what does it tell you about her — is she the first to arrive or the last?" (Last. Which suggests she is not the most important person in the room.)
The most likely wobbleStudents confuse inference with guessing. If they say something the text can't support — "maybe it's her birthday" — don't call it wrong, ask the diagnostic question: "Which words in the sentence made you think that?" When they can't find any, they discover the difference themselves. That question is worth memorising; you will use it a dozen times today.

Finish on the coral box: an inference without a quotation is only an opinion. Have them say the sentence frame back to you — "…because the text says '______'."

4

Read the prologue together

4 min0:09 → 0:13

Screen 4: The Last Dance. Two readings, and they are different jobs.

Reading one — you read it aloud, they listen. Read slowly. Slow down deliberately on "What she did not know was that this would be the last time", and change your voice for the italic note — it is a different person speaking. Then one question only:

Ask

"First feeling. Don't explain it — what did that make you feel?"

Reading two — they read it aloud. This is where they notice things. Expect them to stumble on "coaxed" — good, that is 6Rv.01 arriving on its own.

"Coaxed" means gently persuaded, the way you'd get a nervous cat to come to you. Ask what it suggests about the drumming: that the music is being drawn out of the ground rather than banged out — patient, respectful, old.

Then the plum box at the bottom of the screen: the two halves don't match. Make them spot the pronouns themselves.

Ask

"Look at part one. Which words does it use for Mira — 'I' or 'she'? Now look at the note. Who's talking, and who are they talking to?"

Target: Part 1 is "she" and "her" — someone outside the story telling it. Part 2 is "I" and "you" — someone inside it, speaking directly to a reader. Don't teach first/third person properly here — that is next lesson (Session 1.3). Just let them notice the difference and leave it hanging.
5

Clue hunt — the heart of the lesson

8 min0:13 → 0:21

Screen 5. Eight phrases are highlighted. The procedure matters more than the content: tap nothing until they have committed to an answer.

Set it up

"Every highlighted bit was put there on purpose by the writer. We're going to take them one at a time. You tell me what you think it gives away — and where in the sentence you're getting it from — and then I'll open it and we'll see how close you were."

Eight clues in eight minutes is about a minute each, including talk. That's the right pace. Below is what each one is for, so you can steer without reading the screen.

ClueWhat you're steering them towardsPrompt if stuck
"Three summers before the lights went out"Does two jobs: sets the time, and warns that something bad is coming. This is their first meeting with foreshadowing (named properly in stage 8)."Is that a happy sentence or a worrying one? Which three words do it?"
"the way her grandmother always started"The grandmother isn't in the scene but is in the writing — so she matters. The dance is inherited."Where's the grandmother right now? So why is she in the sentence at all?"
"danced on this beach every year since she could walk"Belonging. The writer is building up how much there is to lose."How much of her life is this beach? So how bad would losing it be?"
"What she did not know…"The big one. Who is speaking? Not Mira. Someone who can see further than she can."If Mira doesn't know it — who does? Who's telling us this story?"
"you found the box, and the shoes are inside it"Objects planted in a prologue always come back. Start an "objects list" habit."Why keep shoes in a box? Why would someone hide them?"
"look for me in the places I never mentioned"Someone is expected to go searching — a journey is coming. And the real places had to stay unspoken."Why would you tell someone to avoid the places you told them about?"
"too many people listening"The best inference in the piece. It never says "I was in danger" — you work it out."Does it say anyone was in danger? So how do you know they were?"
"— M."Could be Mira. Could be her mother or grandmother. The withholding is the hook."Whose name starts with M? Is that the only person it could be?"
If you are running behindClues you must not skip: "What she did not know" (you need it again in stage 8) and "too many people listening" (the cleanest example of inference in the text). The two most droppable are the grandmother and "— M.".
If they are flyingAsk the question that sits above all eight: "Which clue makes you most want to read chapter one, and why that one?" There is no right answer; the reasoning is the point, and it is excellent evidence for 6Rv.04.
6

Mood

5 min0:21 → 0:26

Screen 6. A toggle at the top switches between Part 1 (the beach) and Part 2 (the note); ten mood words below. Tapping a word marks it ✓ or ✗ for the part you are currently on, with a reason.

Define it first

"Mood is the feeling the writing gives you. Not what the characters feel — what you feel while you're reading. And the writer chose it on purpose, one word at a time."

Do Part 1 first (three or four words is plenty — peace, wistfulness and excitement all work), then switch the toggle and do Part 2. The moment worth engineering is when a word that failed on Part 1 passes on Part 2.

Part 1 ✓ peace, wistfulness, excitement, suspense (that last one only because of the final line).
Part 2 ✓ mystery, fear, regret, sorrow, suspense.
Never ✓ anger and hilarity — both are deliberately there as traps.
The trap worth springingAnger is the one to make them tap on Part 2. Lots of students assume a dramatic, secretive note must be angry. Push them back to the actual words — "sorry", "could not", "not enough time". That is sad and frightened, not furious. This is 6Rv.04 in miniature: the writer's word choice is the evidence, not the vibe.

Close on the gold box: two moods in one prologue is deliberate. The warm opening exists so the note feels colder.

Ask

"Why do you think the writer made the beach so lovely, if they knew they were about to scare us?"

7

Change the mood

4 min0:26 → 0:30

Screen 7. The same opening sentence rewritten three times; they match each to Sorrow, Excitement or Suspense. Answers: A = sorrow, B = excitement, C = suspense.

They will get these quickly. The value is entirely in the follow-up question, so ask it every time, before you tap:

Ask, three times

"Which exact words did it? Point at them."

A: "had gone cold", "pulled", "empty" — and the emptiness is the biggest one.
B: "kicked off", "ran", "laughing" — all three are fast verbs where the original was careful.
C: "slid" (quiet, secretive) and the six words held back until the very end: "listening for footsteps behind her".
The transferable pointMake sure they leave with this: the event never changed. A girl takes off her shoes. Everything that changed was word choice. That is what "a writer's choice of language" means in 6Rv.04, and it is what they need to do in their own writing next lesson.

If there is time, one live challenge, no screen: "Give me version D — make me feel peaceful. You can only change three words."

8

Backwards, forwards, all-knowing

4 min0:30 → 0:34

Screen 8. Three definition cards, then three openings to sort. Answers: flashback, foreshadowing, all-knowing narrator, in that order.

Teach the three cards in about ninety seconds. The trick that makes them stick:

Say

"Flashback points backwards — it explains. Foreshadowing points forwards — it warns. And the all-knowing narrator isn't about time at all, it's about who knows what: a voice that knows more than the people in the story do."

OpeningAnswerThe give-away
"Long before any of this…"Flashback"Long before" — backwards.
"One day you will stand in this circle alone…"Foreshadowing"One day you will" — forwards.
"Neither of them noticed the woman watching…"All-knowing narratorIf neither character noticed — who did? Ask that question out loud; it lands every time.

Finish on the dark box, which brings it back to their own text: The Last Dance uses two of the three at once. "What she did not know" is the all-knowing narrator; "three summers before the lights went out" is foreshadowing.

Coursebook linkThis mirrors Activity 1d in Session 1.2, which asks which description matches the prologue in East. If your student also has the coursebook open, run that activity here — the vocabulary transfers exactly.
9

Word origins & prefixes

4 min0:34 → 0:38

Screen 9, and a complete change of gear — from reading a text to taking words apart. Say so, so the switch doesn't feel random.

Say

"You've used the word prologue for two whole lessons. Here's a question you've probably never asked: why is it called that? What does 'prologue' actually mean?"

Walk the tree: pro (before) + logos (word) → prologue, "the words before". Then the line beneath it: epi (in addition) + logos → epilogue, the words after.

Ask before you tap anything

"So if you know logos means 'word' or 'speech' — what do you think a monologue is? What about a dialogue?"

Then the six logos family tiles (tap to open) and the five prefix flip cards. Don't do all eleven. Four or five is plenty in four minutes — the rest are there for the review page and the worksheet.

Priority order if short on time: monologue and dialogue (they connect straight back to logos), then auto (autobiography is the most useful prefix they'll meet all year), then trans and micro. The -ology words can go home with them.
End on the choreography boxChoreography = khoreia (dance) + graphein (to write). A choreographer literally writes a dance down. For a student who dances this is the moment the whole topic turns from a spelling exercise into something that belongs to them — give it ten seconds and let it land. If they enjoy it, offer one more: choreographer and autograph and photograph all share graphein.

Quiz

3 min0:38 → 0:41

Screen 10. Eight questions, a running score at the top. Wrong answers give feedback and stay open, so they can try again — the score only counts correct answers.

#AnswerNote
1Before chapter oneShould be automatic by now.
2A clue from the text plus what you already knowThe lesson's core idea.
3"there were too many people listening"Tests whether they can pick the best evidence, not just any.
4Warm and peaceful, with suspense at the very endThe "at the very end" half is the part that matters.

Recap & homework

Next lessonSession 1.3, Focus on technique: first-person and third-person narrative, why the note is in italics, hyphens and dashes, and building a character in a line. The pronoun question you left hanging in stage 4 is the door straight into it — leave it unanswered on purpose.

If the lesson runs short

Two extensions, both worth real curriculum credit:

  • Rewrite the last line. "What she did not know was that this would be the last time." Ask them to rewrite it so it no longer uses an all-knowing narrator — so it stays inside what Mira herself could know. It is genuinely hard, and it teaches what the technique buys a writer better than any explanation.
  • Plant a clue live. Give them a flat sentence — "She got on the bus." — and ask them to add one detail that makes a reader worry, without saying anything worrying. ("She got on the bus with both suitcases.") Sixty seconds, and it is exactly the skill the worksheet ends on.
