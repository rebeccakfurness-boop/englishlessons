Show Me Who They Are

Lesson 3 · Cambridge Primary English Stage 6, Unit 1: Different voices – different times · Session 1.3 Focus on technique · 40 minutes, one-to-one online

🗣️ Script

Before you start

Lesson 2 made her a reader who hunts for clues. This lesson turns her round: she becomes the writer who plants them. Say that out loud at the start, because it is the thread holding the three techniques together, and without it this looks like three unrelated tricks.

Where this sits. Cambridge Primary English Stage 6 (0058), Unit 1, Session 1.3 Focus on technique. The coursebook goal is: "explore writers' techniques and how to build a picture of a character." Session 1.3 also carries hyphens and dashes as its language focus; that is a whole topic of its own and it is Lesson 4, not today.
  • 6Rv.04: "Comment on a writer's choice of language, demonstrating some awareness of the impact on the reader." This is the spine of the whole lesson.
  • 6Ri.14: "Support answers to questions with reference to, or quotations from, one or more points in a text." Carried over from Lesson 2: keep asking for the words.
An honest note on codes: Cambridge has specific objectives for narrative voice and for composing character description, but I could not reach the framework document to quote them exactly, so I have not cited codes I cannot verify. The two above are quoted verbatim. Check them against your own copy before any formal record.
Teaching this to a young learner working in her second languageThe whole lesson is built out of doing rather than explaining, because that is what holds a ten-year-old. Three of the eleven screens are games. Your job on those is mostly to shut up and let her play, then ask one question at the end of each.

Pre-teach three words before you start: narrator, emphasis, italics. Everything else she can meet in context. Let her answer in short phrases; you are not assessing her spoken English today.

The showing sentences in stage 7 use her own four describing words from her worksheet: angry, silent, happy, crazy. Point that out. It matters more than you would think that the material is hers.
The one habit to keepSame as last lesson: "Which words made you think that?" Today it works in both directions. When she reads, it finds the evidence. When she writes, it makes her notice her own choices. Use it after every single game.

1

Welcome: you are the writer now

2 min0:00 → 0:02

Screen 1. Read the three tricks in the teal box together, then go to the dark box on the right.

Say

"Last lesson you were the detective. You found the clues somebody else had hidden. Today we swap over. Today you are the one hiding them."

"And remember that thing you spotted last time, where part one said she and the note said I? I never told you why. That is where we are starting."

This callback is worth doing properlyYou deliberately left that question unanswered at the end of Lesson 2. Paying it off now tells her the lessons are one continuous thing, not a series of separate worksheets.
2

Warm-up: still got it?

3 min0:02 → 0:05

Four flip cards. She answers out loud, then you tap.

CardWhat you want
InferenceClue plus what you already know. If she says only "a guess", push back: what has to come first?
EvidenceA quotation. She should be quick on this one by now.
MoodThe reader's feeling, not the character's. The most commonly muddled of the three.
NarratorNew word, so let her guess. Anything near "the person telling it" is a win. Do not correct "the writer" harshly, just say "close, and today you will see why they are not always the same".
3

Teach: who is telling it?

3 min0:05 → 0:08

Screen 3. Two cards, first person and third person. Keep this short: the game on the next screen does the real teaching.

Say

"Every story is told by somebody, and a writer gets to choose where that somebody stands. Inside the character's head, or outside watching them."

Then connect it straight back: the gold box reminds her that an all-knowing narrator is third person doing its special trick. Ask: "Could you write 'What she did not know was...' if the story was told by I?" Let her think. The answer is no, and working that out herself is the moment the concept lands.
4

Play: the swapper

5 min0:08 → 0:13

Screen 4. Two buttons, She and I, and one passage that rewrites itself. The five words that change are highlighted in gold in both versions.

Do this

Let her press the buttons herself, several times. Do not narrate over it. Then ask the three questions below, in order.

AskWhat you are listening for
"How many words actually changed?"Five. They are all pronouns. Nothing else in the passage moved at all.
"Which one feels closer to you?"Almost everyone says I. Ask why. Target: because you are the one carrying the box, not watching somebody carry it.
"So when would a writer choose she?"The hard question. When you need to tell the reader something the character does not know, or show more than one person.
Notice the passageIt uses "the forest was silent", which is hers, from her own worksheet. If she spots it, brilliant. If not, tell her at the end of the stage.
5

Teach: the loud word

2 min0:13 → 0:15

Screen 5. Ninety seconds of talking, no more. The game is next and it explains itself.

Say, and demonstrate with your voice

"When you talk, you make one word louder to change what you mean. Listen: I never said that. I never said that."

"On paper you cannot be loud. So writers lean the word over instead. That is what italics are for."

Say those two sentences aloud with real emphasisShe needs to hear the difference before she sees it. This is the one stage where your voice does more than the screen.
6

Play: seven words, seven meanings

6 min0:15 → 0:21

Screen 6, and the best thing in the lesson. One sentence, "I never said she took my shoes." Tapping any word makes it the loud one and prints what the sentence now means. Seven words, seven completely different meanings, and a row of pips that fills up as she finds them.

Set it up, then get out of the way

"This sentence has seven words. It also has seven completely different meanings, and I have not changed a single letter. Find all seven."

Make her say each one out loudThis is the difference between a nice animation and actual learning. After each tap: "Now say it the way that means that." She has to perform the emphasis with her voice. If she just reads the explanation silently, the stage is wasted.
Loud wordWhat it does
ISomebody said it, but not me. You have the wrong person.
neverNot once, not ever. A total denial.
saidI did not say it out loud. I might have thought it, or written it.
sheSomebody took them and I said so, but it was not her.
tookArgues with that one word. Maybe she borrowed them, or moved them.
myShe took shoes. They were not mine.
shoesShe took something of mine, but not the shoes.
The question that closes the stage"So if you write a sentence and you do not tell the reader which word is loud, how do they know?" They do not. That is exactly why writers reach for italics.
7

Teach: show, do not tell

3 min0:21 → 0:24

Screen 7. Two cards side by side: "She was angry" against "She put the cup down harder than she meant to."

Ask first, do not explain first

"Both of these tell you she is angry. Which one do you actually believe? And why?"

Target: the second, because you worked it out yourself. Nobody argues with a conclusion they reached on their own. If she says the first because it is clearer: fair, and worth agreeing with. Clearer is not the same as stronger. The first is a label; the second is a picture.
The line that ties the unit togetherThe dark box says it: showing is inference from the other side. Last lesson she dug clues up. Today she buries them. Say it in those words, because it turns two lessons into one idea.
8

Play: her own words

5 min0:24 → 0:29

Screen 8. Four rounds, one for each of the describing words she wrote on her worksheet: angry, silent, happy, crazy. Each round gives three sentences and she picks the one that shows rather than tells.

Open with

"These four words are yours. You wrote them. Now we are going to turn each one into a picture."

WordAnswerThe teaching point
angry"He put the plate in the sink without looking at anyone."The word angry never appears. Ask her to check. That surprises students every time.
silent"I could hear my own breathing, and nothing else."The clever one. You cannot hear silence, so you show it by naming the one sound left.
happy"She took the stairs two at a time."Happy people move differently. One action carries it.
crazy"He spun round three times, then bowed to a tree."Worth a word here: "crazy" on its own tells a reader nothing, and it is a lazy label about a person. A specific odd action is both kinder and better writing.
If she is finding these easyStop tapping and make her produce one. "Give me a showing sentence for 'frightened'. You are not allowed the word frightened." Sixty seconds. It is much harder than choosing, and it is exactly what the worksheet asks her to do at home.
9

Play: the character machine

6 min0:29 → 0:35

Screen 9. Three rows of three: what they carry, what their hands are doing, what they never do. Pick one from each and the machine writes a character paragraph underneath.

Do this

Build one together first, then let her rebuild it two or three times with different choices. Read each version out loud in a storyteller voice. This should be fun.

The three questions, in this order 1. "Who is this person? Tell me about them." She will invent a whole backstory. Let her.
2. "Now find me the word in there that says how they feel." There is not one. Let her hunt for it.
3. "So how did you know all that?" She inferred it, from three details. That is the whole lesson, proved with her own example.
Why these three categories: an object, a habit of the hands, and an absence. The third is the strongest and the least obvious. What a person never does characterises them faster than almost anything else, and it is a trick she can take straight into her own writing.
If you are short of timeThis is the stage to shorten, not to cut. One build instead of three, and skip straight to the three questions. Cutting it entirely would remove the moment the lesson is for.
10

Quiz

4 min0:35 → 0:39

Screen 10. Six questions with a running score. Wrong answers explain and stay open, so she can try again.

#AnswerNote
1I, me, myStraight recall.
2Third personThe one that proves she understood the swapper, not just enjoyed it.
3To make it the loud word, or show a different voiceBoth jobs. Accept either half in conversation.
4"She checked the door was locked three times."Note that the distractor "Nervousness came over her" is still telling, just dressed up. Worth pointing at.
5The reader works it out, so they believe itThe most important question on the page.
6She did something with them, but taking is the wrong wordApplies the emphasis game rather than recalling it.
11

Recap and homework

1 min0:39 → 0:40

Screen 11. Six vocabulary cards. Point at the three that are genuinely new: narrator, emphasis, show do not tell.

Close with

"Worksheet 3 is the one you print out, same as last time. You will swap a passage, pick the loud word, turn four telling sentences into showing ones, and build your own character."

"And here is the rule for the rest of your life as a writer: if you catch yourself writing 'she was sad', stop, and show me instead."

Next lessonSession 1.3's language focus: hyphens and dashes. Hyphens glue words into one idea (eleven-year-old, sugar-free, runner-up); a dash makes the reader stop right before the good bit. You already have a page on this from her earlier sequence, so check whether it needs rebuilding or just reordering before you plan Lesson 4.

If the lesson runs short

  • Break the machine on purpose. Give her a bad detail to add: "she was feeling quite sad about everything". Put it into the character paragraph and read the whole thing aloud. Ask what it did. It flattens it. One telling sentence can undo three showing ones, and hearing that is worth more than being told it.
  • Emphasis, out loud, no screen. You say a sentence with one word loud, she says what you meant. Then swap. Thirty seconds each way, and it is genuinely funny.
