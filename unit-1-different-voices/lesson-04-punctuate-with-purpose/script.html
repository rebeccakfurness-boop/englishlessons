Plan Your Prologue

Lesson 4 · Cambridge Primary English Stage 6, Unit 1 · Session 1.4: plan, write and edit a prologue · 40 minutes, one-to-one online

🗣️ Script

Before you start

This lesson is different from the first three, and you need to hold that difference in your head or it will drift. The other lessons taught her something. This one produces something. You are not delivering content today, you are running a planning conversation and typing down what she says.

By the end there is a finished plan on screen with fourteen decisions in it. She takes that away and writes the prologue at home. That split matters: planning is the hard part, and you are doing the hard part with her so that the writing itself is almost easy.

Straight from her Learner's Book. Session 1.4 says: "plan, write and edit a prologue", and activity 1a is "Use a planning diagram and jot down notes on what your prologue will include." The on-screen planner is that planning diagram. Activity 1b lists six techniques for the draft, quoted verbatim:
  • carefully chosen words to create the mood you want
  • clear narrative voice (third-person or first-person)
  • consistent tense (only change it for dramatic effect)
  • variety of short and long sentences
  • italics for effect
  • dashes and semicolons
Those six are screen 9, in that order. This is also where the punctuation from earlier finally has a job: a dash is not a rule to learn, it is the tool that makes her last line land.
The single biggest risk in this lessonYou filling in the blanks yourself. It will be tempting, because the idea buttons are right there and silence is uncomfortable. Do not take an idea until she has been stuck for a good ten seconds. Her worst own idea is worth more than your best one, because she will actually want to write it. The buttons exist for genuine stuck, not for quiet.
How to typeType what she says, in her words, not tidied up. If she says "a girl who's always last out the door", type that, not "a punctual-averse protagonist". She has to recognise her own voice in the plan or she will not feel it is hers.

The page saves to the browser as you go, so if the tab reloads mid-lesson nothing is lost. It does not reach you afterwards, so screenshot the final plan on screen 10 before you finish, and send it to her with the worksheet.
Teaching this to a young learner in her second languageThis lesson is mostly her talking, which is harder than tapping. Two adjustments: accept single words and build the sentence for her out loud ("cold? cold how? cold like the gate handle?"), and let her answer the five senses in any order, starting with whichever comes easiest. Sound is usually easiest. Taste is always hardest, and it is fine to leave it blank.

Welcome: today we plan your story

2 min0:00 → 0:02
Say

"Everything we have done so far has been about somebody else's writing. Today we make yours. We are going to plan a whole story opening together, right now, and by the end of the lesson it will all be on this screen."

"Then you go home and write it. And here is the thing: you will not be staring at an empty page, because every decision will already be made."

Set the expectation that she talksSay it plainly: "I am going to do the typing. You do the thinking." It changes how she sits.
2

Warm-up: everything you already have

3 min0:02 → 0:05

Four flip cards: sensory language, inference, show do not tell, mood. Not new content, deliberately. The point is to show her she is not starting from nothing.

Close the warm-up with

"Four tools, and today you use all four at once. That is what a real writer does."

3

Who, where, when

5 min0:05 → 0:10

Three boxes, each with an idea button. This is the stage that decides whether she is invested, so spend the time here rather than rushing to the senses.

If she saysDo this
"A girl."Push once, gently. "What kind of girl? Tell me one thing about her that nobody else knows." One specific detail is worth more than a name.
"I don't know."Wait. Count to ten in your head. Then offer the idea button, and make her change at least one word of whatever it gives her.
Somewhere invented and enormousLet her have it, but anchor it: "Fine. What does it smell like there?" A vast invented place she cannot picture will kill the senses stage.
Something from her own lifeBest possible outcome. Take it and do not dress it up.
Why "when" matters more than it looksAn exact time is a free clue. "Last night, just before the rain" already suggests something. "One day" suggests nothing. If she gives you something vague, ask for the weather.
4

The five senses

6 min0:10 → 0:16

Five boxes, one per sense, each with its own idea button. The longest stage in the lesson, and the one that will most improve her writing.

Frame it

"This is the bit that puts your reader inside the place. Not big dramatic things. Small, exact, true things."

Reject the first answer if it is a category"Trees" is a category. "The trees moving when there is no wind" is a detail. The question that fixes it every time: "Which trees? Doing what?" Keep asking until it is something she can actually see.
Order to run them in: hearing first, it is easiest and it warms her up. Then sight, touch, smell. Taste last, and tell her outright that leaving it blank is allowed. Chasing a taste detail for a forest scene wastes two minutes and sours the stage.
Tie it backHer very first lesson was the five senses video. Say so. "Remember your first ever lesson with me? This is that, except now it is for your own story."
5

Build your character

4 min0:16 → 0:20

The three questions from the character machine in Lesson 3, now for her real character: what they carry, what their hands do, what they never do.

She has done this before, so it should be quickIf she is slow, remind her of the machine. If she is fast, push on the third one: "never" is the strongest of the three and the one most worth getting right.
Check as you type: none of the three may name a feeling. If she says "their hands are shaking because they are scared", take the shaking and drop the reason. That edit, made in front of her, teaches show-not-tell better than any explanation.
6

Your secret

4 min0:20 → 0:24

Two boxes: the secret, and the one clue that points at it. This is the heart of the whole unit, arriving from the writer's side.

Say

"A prologue works because of something it does not tell you. So: what do you know about your story that your reader is not allowed to know yet?"

Hold the line on the second boxThe clue must be a thing, not an explanation. If she writes "you can tell she is leaving", that is not a clue, it is a summary. Ask: "What would I actually SEE that tells me that?" Keep pushing until it is an object or an action. This is the most valuable sixty seconds in the lesson.
Worked example if she needs one: secret, "she has already packed". Clue, "a bag under the bed with the zip half open". She has read that one. Point out that the story never once says she is leaving.
7

Voice and mood

3 min0:24 → 0:27

Two rows of buttons. Quick, but make her justify each one.

AskWhat you want
"First person or third? And why that one?"Any reason is fine as long as it is a reason. If she wants an all-knowing narrator telling us something the character does not know, she needs third.
"What should the reader feel?"One word. Then say the useful bit: "Now every word you write either pushes towards that feeling or fights it. You have just made every choice easier."
8

The shape

4 min0:27 → 0:31

Hook, build, turn. Three boxes. Notes, not sentences, except possibly the hook.

Hook: the first line. Worth writing out in full, because a good first line makes the rest pour out at home.
Build: where the senses go. Notes are fine.
Turn: the last line, where the clue lands. Tell her the trick: make it short. Much shorter than the sentences before it.
If she is flyingGet the hook and the turn written out as real sentences here, in the lesson. Two good sentences at the top and bottom, and the middle almost writes itself.
9

Six tools for when you write

4 min0:31 → 0:35

The Learner's Book's own six techniques, in its order. Two have a play button: short and long sentences, and dashes. Both hold the last few words back and then drop them.

Play both, and connect them to her turnShe has just decided her last line on the previous screen. Play the dash demo, then say: "That is what a dash could do to your turn." Punctuation stops being a rule and becomes a thing she wants.
Do not teach all six. Point at them, play the two demos, and tell her the list is there to keep beside her while she writes. It is a reference, not a lecture, and the worksheet has it as a tick list.
10

Read the plan back

4 min0:35 → 0:39

Screen 10 assembles everything into one panel, with a count of how many decisions are made.

Do this, it is the best moment of the lesson

Read the whole plan out loud to her, slowly, like you are telling her about a story you have just read. Then: "You made all of that up. In half an hour."

Before you end the callScreenshot this screen and send it to her. The plan saves in her browser, not to you, and if she opens the page on a different device it will be empty. The screenshot is the safety net, and it is also what she writes from.
Gaps are fineIf two or three rows say "not decided yet", leave them. Tell her they are hers to decide while she writes. A plan with a little room left in it is more inviting than a completely closed one.
11

What to do at home

1 min0:39 → 0:40
Close with

"Two or three paragraphs. That is all a prologue is. You already know who, where, when, what it smells like, who your person is and what you are hiding."

"And when you have written it, read it out loud to somebody and ask them what they think the secret is. If they guess something close, your clue worked. That is the real test."

Next lessonSession 1.4 finishes with reviewing: reading it aloud, checking whether the clues were picked up, then fixing word choice, spelling and punctuation. So next time, bring her draft and edit it together. That is also where the hyphens and dashes get used on her own writing rather than on exercises.

If the lesson runs short

  • Write the first line together, properly. Not notes, the actual sentence, out loud, three or four attempts until one of them sounds right. Nothing else you can do in five minutes will help her more at home.
  • Play the guessing game. You read her plan back but leave out the secret, and she checks whether the clue alone points at it. If you cannot guess from the clue, the clue is too quiet. If you guess instantly, it is too loud.
