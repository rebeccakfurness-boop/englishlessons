Short Line, Long Line

Lesson 4 · Cambridge Primary English Stage 6, Unit 1 · Session 1.3 Language focus: hyphens and dashes · 40 minutes, one-to-one online

🗣️ Script

Before you start

This is the punctuation half of Session 1.3, and the last lesson of Unit 1. It is easy to teach badly, because "a hyphen is short and a dash is long" is true, boring, and forgettable. The lesson is built on a different idea: the hyphen is glue and the dash is a gap. One sticks words together, the other pulls them apart. Everything hangs off that.

Straight from her books. Both the Learner's Book and the Workbook carry the same Language focus box for Session 1.3, quoted here as they print it:
  • "A hyphen is a short line that links words together to create one idea, called a compound word." Examples given: eleven-year-old, sugar-free, twenty-nine, two-thirds, runner-up.
  • "Dashes are longer than hyphens. A single dash can signal: a dramatic pause leading to a climax or anti-climax; an aside or comment; additional information or contrast." Example given: She paused – then shouted 'Hooray!'
The three jobs taught on screen 5 are exactly those three, in the book's own order.
The Workbook pairs with this lesson directlyWorkbook page 12 has Session 1.3 in three tiers, and this lesson follows the same shape so the book feels familiar rather than extra:

Focus (choose hyphen or dash) is screen 8 here. Practice (add information using dashes) is screen 9. Challenge (say what each dash signals) is screen 7.

The sentences on screen are mine, not the book's, so you can set the Workbook exercises afterwards as genuinely fresh practice. If you have the physical book to hand, open it at the end and let her do Focus 1 straight away while it is warm.
One thing to get right yourself before you teach itA hyphen has no spaces around it: ten-year-old. A dash has a space each side: silent – then. That spacing is most of how a reader tells them apart, and she will copy whatever you write in the chat window during the lesson. Watch your own typing.
Teaching this to a young learner in her second languagePunctuation is abstract, so this lesson makes both marks physical: one visibly snaps words together, the other visibly makes her wait. Let the animations do that work.

Pre-teach two words: compound and anti-climax. The second is worth a joke rather than a definition. Build something up, then say "and inside the box was... a sock." She will get it immediately.

1

Welcome: two marks, two jobs

2 min0:00 → 0:02

Screen 1 shows both marks at large size, side by side. Let her look at them before you say anything.

Ask first

"What is the difference between those two?"

She will say "one is longer". Accept it, then give her the useful version: "The short one is glue. The long one is a gap." That sentence is the whole lesson, and she should be able to repeat it back at the end.
2

Warm-up

3 min0:02 → 0:05

Four flip cards from Lesson 3: first person, italics, show do not tell, and compound as the new word to guess.

On the compound cardLet her guess properly. "Compound" is used in science too, and if she knows that meaning, it transfers: two things joined to make one new thing. That is exactly what a hyphen does to words.
3

Teach: the hyphen is glue

2 min0:05 → 0:07

Screen 3 contrasts a ten year old girl with a ten-year-old girl. Read both aloud, deliberately stumbling over the first.

Say

"Without the glue your eye trips over three separate words. With it, your eye reads one idea and moves on."

The number rule is the one that pays off in tests: every number from twenty-one to ninety-nine takes a hyphen, and so do fractions like two-thirds. It comes up in the quiz and on the worksheet.

5

Teach: the dash is a gap

2 min0:07 → 0:14

Screen 5. Three coloured cards, one per job, in the book's own order: dramatic pause, aside, extra information. Ninety seconds. Do not over-explain, the games do it.

Say

"A dash has a space on each side, because it is the opposite of glue. It opens a hole in the sentence, and your reader falls into it for half a second."

6

Play: the pause machine

6 min0:14 → 0:20

Screen 6, and the best thing in the lesson. One sentence, "The forest was silent, then the drums started." Two buttons switch between the comma version and the dash version, and Play it reveals the sentence with a real timed pause. The comma waits a quarter of a second. The dash waits well over a second, and the screen shows dots while you wait.

Run it in this order

Comma version first, play it. Then dash version, play it. Then ask.

AskWhat you are after
"Which one made you want to know what came next?"The dash. The waiting itself created the wanting.
"Did any of the words change?"No. Not one. Only the mark, and how long she was held there.
"So what is a dash actually for?"Target: making the reader wait on purpose. That is the definition worth keeping.
Run it a third time with her reading aloudHave her read the dash version out loud and actually hold the silence. Most children rush straight through it. Making her hold a real pause with her own voice is what connects the mark on the page to the effect in the ear.
7

Play: name the dash's job

5 min0:20 → 0:25

Screen 7. This is the Workbook's Challenge tier. Four sentences, three possible jobs.

SentenceAnswerThe give-away
She ran all the way to the gate – then stopped.Dramatic pauseOne dash, near the end, with the big moment after it.
My friend Amina – the one who moved away in June – sent me a letter.AsideTwo dashes. Teach the test: lift out the middle and see whether the sentence still works. It does.
I packed everything I needed – my torch, my map and a jumper.Extra informationA list that adds detail. Nothing dramatic about it.
I lifted the lid, and inside the box was – nothing at all.Dramatic pauseAn anti-climax. Same technique, opposite payoff, and it is meant to be funny.
The counting shortcutIf she is struggling, give her this: two dashes means an aside, nearly always. One dash means she has to think about what comes after it.
8

Play: glue or gap?

5 min0:25 → 0:30

Screen 8, the Workbook's Focus tier. Five sentences with a gap, and two buttons. Answers: hyphen, dash, hyphen, dash, hyphen.

Give her the one question that solves all five

"Am I sticking two words together, or am I making you wait?"

The best one to linger on is number 2: "I could hear something behind me – footsteps, getting closer." Ask what a hyphen would do there. It would glue "me" to "footsteps" and make "me-footsteps", which is nonsense. That absurdity is the clearest proof of the difference in the whole lesson.
9

Play: drop the dashes in

4 min0:30 → 0:34

Screen 9, the Workbook's Practice tier, turned into something clickable. A sentence with an extra bit pushed into the middle, and tappable gaps between every word. She taps the two gaps where the dashes belong. Two taps and it tells her whether she is right.

Teach the lift-out test before she taps"Read it without the extra bit. 'My school is the biggest in the town.' Now, where did you lift it out from? That is where the dashes go." That test works for every aside she will ever punctuate, and it is the single most useful thing on this screen.
If she puts them in the wrong place, do not correct her. Make her read her own version aloud including the dashes. She will hear that the sentence has broken, and fix it herself.

Recap and homework

1 min0:39 → 0:40

Six vocabulary cards. Then point out that this is the end of Unit 1.

Close the unit

"Look what you can do now. You can spot a prologue, read one like a detective, build a character nobody has ever met, and punctuate it so your reader feels every pause. That is a whole unit."

"Worksheet 4 has the same three steps as your Cambridge workbook: Focus, Practice, Challenge. Then you write three sentences of your own, one for each job a dash can do."

Set the Workbook too, if you have itWorkbook page 12 is now genuinely easy practice rather than new work, because every shape on it has been played on screen. It is the cheapest extra homework you will ever set.

If the lesson runs short

  • The anti-climax game. You start a sentence dramatically, she finishes it with something deliberately boring. "I opened the door, and standing there was – the postman." Take turns. It teaches timing, and it is the one thing from this lesson she will still be doing next week for fun.
  • Hyphen hunt. Give her sixty seconds to find a hyphen anywhere: a book, a food packet, the screen. Then work out together what two ideas it is gluing.
  • Semicolons, if she is flying. Activity 1e in the Learner's Book asks about semicolons in the East extract. It is the only place they appear in this session and there is no Language focus teaching them, so treat it as an extension, not as content she is expected to have.
