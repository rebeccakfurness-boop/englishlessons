🗣️ Teaching Script

Cut, Sharpen, Vary, Check

Lesson 11 · Cambridge Lower Secondary English, Unit 1B · Written for a tutor teaching this for the first time

↩ Lesson 10 🕹️ Interactive lesson 🗣️ Script

Before you start

Tom arrives at this lesson holding something he has never had before in this unit: a finished first draft of his own. Lesson 10 got him to plan and write it. This lesson is about the part almost every student skips: going back in and making it better. He will resist cutting his own sentences. That resistance is the lesson.

Real curriculum reference: this lesson targets Cambridge objective 8Wp.04: "evaluate and edit to improve the accuracy and effectiveness of language, grammar and structure of a range of different texts." Read that objective carefully: it names two separate things, accuracy and effectiveness. The whole lesson is built on keeping those two jobs apart and doing them in the right order.
The one idea this whole lesson hangs on Editing and proofreading are not the same job. Editing asks "does this work?" Proofreading asks "is anything wrong?" Students conflate them, so they "redraft" by fixing three spellings and changing nothing that matters. If Tom leaves today understanding that distinction and nothing else, the lesson has succeeded.
He must have his Lesson 10 draft with him Stage 7 is unteachable without it. Check at the very start of the call, before Stage 0. If he hasn't got it, use the model draft in Stage 4 as his working text for Stage 7 instead, and set the real edit as homework.

SHARPEN

0

What & Why

0-3 minSet up the shift from producing writing to improving it. Be direct that first drafts are supposed to be imperfect, which defuses any defensiveness before you ask him to cut his own work.

Say this
"Last lesson you planned and drafted your opening. Today you do the thing that actually separates decent writing from good writing: you go back and make it better. Not by adding more. By making every word earn its place."
Say this
"Nobody's first draft is their best work, and it isn't supposed to be. That's what a draft is for. Professional writers spend more time editing than drafting."
1

Warm-up: Recall the Toolkit

3-7 minFive flip cards. Four are revision (the four moves, show-don't-tell, honest detail, active/passive). The fifth, redundancy, is new vocabulary he is meant to guess at, so let him try before flipping it.

Say this
"Five things you already know, all of which you're about to use. Say your answer out loud before you tap."
Keep this brisk This is recall, not re-teaching. If an answer is shaky, flip the card, restate it in one sentence and move on. The real teaching starts at Stage 2. If you overrun here you will eat into Stage 7, which is where the value is.
2

Teach: Editing Is Not Proofreading

7-11 minTwo side-by-side boxes. This is the conceptual heart of the lesson and everything downstream depends on it landing.

Say this
"Most people use these two words to mean the same thing. They don't. Editing asks: does this writing work? Proofreading asks: is anything actually wrong? Different questions, different jobs."
Ask him
"When you've 'checked over' a piece of writing in the past, which of these two were you actually doing?"

Almost every student answers "proofreading", and realising that is the point. Let him say it himself rather than telling him.

Say this
"Always edit first, proofread last. There is no point perfecting the punctuation of a sentence you're about to delete."
3

Teach: The Four Lenses

11-16 minFour flip cards introducing the method: read the draft four separate times, hunting one thing each time.

Say this
"Instead of reading your draft once and vaguely hoping to spot problems, you read it four times, looking for one thing each time. It sounds slower. It's actually much faster, because you're only ever asking yourself one question."
LensThe point to land
CutThe fastest improvement available, and the one students resist hardest. Filler words, repeated ideas, sentences that explain what the reader already got.
SharpenVague → exact. This is also where "show, don't tell" from Lesson 6 gets applied as a concrete editing move rather than an abstract principle.
VarySentence length and openings. Must be done by reading aloud, because you cannot hear rhythm silently.
CheckThe proofread. The only lens that isn't about making the writing better, just about making sure nothing is broken.
Make the order explicit Say out loud: "The first three are editing. The fourth is proofreading. That's why Check comes last." Tie it straight back to Stage 2 so the structure of the method visibly follows from the distinction he just learned.
4

Model: One Draft, Four Lenses

16-23 minThe centrepiece. A deliberately weak first draft and its edited version, with a toggle between them and seven tappable changes. Give this stage the time it deserves.

How to run this stage Read the first draft aloud together, in full, before you tap anything. Ask him what's wrong with it before revealing the answers. Then work through the highlighted stretches one at a time, but let him predict each fix before you open the annotation.
Say this
"Read this out loud with me. It's not terrible. It's exactly what a real first draft looks like. So, what's the first thing you'd change?"
#LensWhat to draw out
1SharpenThe word "nervous" disappears entirely in the edit. Ask him to find it. He can't, because the edit shows the feeling instead. That's show-don't-tell as a concrete move.
2CutAn entire sentence deleted with nothing lost. Ask: "what does this sentence tell us that the one before it didn't?" Answer: nothing.
3Sharpen"A lot of people" → "three hundred people". Push the general principle: an exact number is always more convincing than a vague quantity.
4SharpenThe honest version (Arif says nothing) is more interesting than the tidy one. Links straight back to Lesson 6 on honest detail.
5VaryThe big one. One long flat sentence becomes "I came fifth." plus a longer sentence. Read both aloud, and he should hear the difference.
6CheckA tense slip and a misspelling. Use this to prove why Check is last: neither error changes what the writing does.
7CutThe tidy moral gets deleted. The honest, unresolved ending is stronger. Hardest idea in the lesson, so see below.
Change 7 will get pushback Students are taught for years that a piece of writing should end with a lesson learned, so deleting "it taught me that winning isn't everything" feels like breaking a rule. Don't rush past it. Read both endings aloud and ask which one sounds like a real person. Then point out that the edited version still shows the change (he went back the next week), it just refuses to announce it.
Finish the stage with this
"Look at the word count. The edited version is shorter than the draft. Editing is not the same as adding."
5

Practice: Which Lens Catches This?

23-28 minEight diagnostic problems, sorted into one of the four lenses. Tests whether the method transferred out of the model and into his own judgement.

Say this
"Eight problems from other students' drafts. Which of the four lenses catches each one?"
The two to slow down on Item 1 ("very cold" / "really cold") legitimately touches both Cut and Sharpen. The marked answer is Cut, but if he says Sharpen, tell him he's half right and explain the sequence: cut the duplicate first, then sharpen what's left. Item 7 ("the food was nice") is the one students most often misread as Cut; the point is that the detail should be replaced, not deleted.
6

Have a Go: The Upgrade Lab

28-32 minThree rounds, three candidate sentences each, one genuinely stronger. Hook, then Build detail, then Land.

Say this
"Three rounds. Only one option in each is genuinely stronger. Tell me why before you tap."

The "why" matters far more than the choice here. He can get all three right by instinct and still not be able to apply it to his own writing; making him articulate the reason is what transfers.

Round 3 is the one that teaches Both wrong answers are the endings students actually write: the cliché ("an experience I'll never forget") and the essay voice ("in conclusion, markets are an important part of..."). Name them as the two standard traps, and point out the second isn't life writing at all, because it's slipped into a different genre.
7

Now His Turn: Four Passes Over His Own Draft

32-37 minThe payoff. Four prompt cards, one per lens, applied live to his own Lesson 10 draft while he marks changes onto Worksheet 11.

Say this
"Get your draft in front of you. We're going to go through it four times, and each time we're only looking for one thing."
PassWhat to actually do on the call
CutHave him read aloud and stop at every very, really, quite, a lot. Cross them out, don't replace yet. Then ask whether any whole sentence repeats an earlier one.
SharpenPick his two vaguest phrases and interrogate them: "how many, exactly?" "what did it actually smell like?" "what did she actually say?" He usually knows. He just didn't write it down.
VaryHe reads aloud, you listen for flatness. Find his single most important moment and ask him to give it a sentence under five words. Check for four consecutive sentences opening with "I".
CheckOnly now. If time is short, set this pass as homework rather than cutting Sharpen or Vary, because it's the pass he can most reliably do alone.
He will not want to cut his own writing This is universal and worth naming out loud: "It feels like losing work. It isn't. You're keeping the good sentences and giving them more room." If he genuinely won't delete something, ask him to read the paragraph aloud with and without it, and let his own ear decide.
Teach the backwards proofread Reading the last sentence first and working upwards stops the eye autocorrecting text it expects to see. It's a real professional technique, students find it genuinely surprising, and it costs ten seconds to explain.
8

Quick Check

37-39 minSeven quickfire questions. Run it fast, and if he's slow on any, that's the thing to reinforce in the recap.

Say this
"Quickfire, before you finish this at home."

Question 1 (editing vs proofreading) and question 2 (which lens is last) are the two that matter. If either is wrong, re-state the Stage 2 distinction before you let him go.

9

Recap & Homework

39-40 minVocabulary recap, hand off to Worksheet 11 and the review activity, and forward-link to next lesson.

Say this
"That's the full writing cycle now: plan, draft, edit, proofread. On Google Classroom you've got a short review activity and Worksheet 11: diagnose eight problems, rewrite three weak sentences, then run all four passes over your own draft properly."
Say this
"The last question on the worksheet is the important one: tell me the single change that improved your draft most, and why. That's you evaluating your own writing, which is exactly what the Cambridge objective asks for."
Say this
"Next time we put all of it under exam conditions: an unseen passage, and writing to a time limit."
